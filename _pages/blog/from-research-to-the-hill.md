<p class="page__subtitle" style="font-size: 0.9em; color: var(--muted); margin-top: -0.5em; margin-bottom: 1em;">Reflections on the UC Presidential Faculty Fellows Program, May 2026</p>

This week I am in Washington, D.C. as part of the [UC Presidential Faculty Fellows Program](https://www.ucdc.edu/faculty-opportunities), run by the [UC Washington Center (UCDC)](https://www.ucdc.edu/). The program selects a small number of UC faculty each year to present their research to congressional staffers and policymakers.

My topic: how to build independent broadband data for public accountability. The [policy brief](/pdfs/policy-brief-v18.pdf) lays out the case. The federal government has invested over $100 billion in broadband. The data used to track these investments comes from the providers themselves. When our team [independently audited](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3651890.3672271) over 500,000 addresses in the Connect America Fund, we found that providers failed to deliver at 45 percent of subsidized locations. With $42 billion now flowing through the BEAD program, we need data infrastructure that can verify whether these investments are working.

## Writing for a different audience

I have spent the past several weeks learning how to say this in 700 words and ten minutes, without slides, to people who handle six other policy areas.

In an academic paper, the contribution is the method. The policy implication gets a paragraph in the conclusion. In a policy brief, the method is background. The action item is the point. A congressional staffer who reads your brief and thinks "that was interesting" but cannot identify a next step will move on to the next meeting.

My first draft read like a condensed research paper. It led with methodology, used acronyms freely, and buried the recommendations. Over multiple rounds of revision, with coaching from [SSN](https://scholars.org/) and input from [Tejas Narechania](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/our-faculty/faculty-profiles/tejas-narechania/) at Berkeley Law, the brief changed substantially. A few things I would not have figured out on my own:

The opening paragraph matters more than anything else. "Taxpayers deserve to know if $42 billion is delivering" connects with a staffer. "Our serviceability rate was 55.45%" does not. The data supports the argument; the values carry it.

Recommendations have to be specific and legislative. Jessica Thompson, a former nine-year Hill staffer now at SSN, was blunt: every researcher says "fund more research." She is right. I anchored the brief in [S. 4448](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4448), a bill Senator Thune introduced the same week as my coaching session, and framed independent data as something that makes existing oversight bills work, not as a new ask.

The same finding serves different framings. The CAF audit can be presented as fiscal waste (a provider took $1 billion and met standards at 16 percent of addresses) or as a rural equity failure (the most remote communities were left behind). A bipartisan audience needs to hear both.

One surprise: Jessica flagged that heavy use of em dashes signals AI-generated text to experienced Hill staffers. The final version has zero.

## The program

UCDC, led by [Tanya Golash-Boza](https://sociology.ucmerced.edu/content/tanya-golash-boza), organized the week: workshops, housing, briefing logistics. SSN, led by Shannon Kelly and Jessica Thompson, ran the coaching: two virtual training sessions, one-on-one calls, a [policy brief writing guide](https://scholars.org/briefs), meeting request templates, and a database of staffer contacts. Without this, I would not have known which staffer in which office handles broadband, or what to expect inside a Senate office building.

## What happens this week

The briefing is Thursday afternoon in the Dirksen Senate Office Building, alongside [Nadia Brashier](https://www.nadiabrashier.com/) (UC San Diego, misinformation) and [Nicol Turner Lee](https://www.brookings.edu/people/nicol-turner-lee/) (Brookings, AI equity). The session is co-sponsored by Congressman Adam Gray.

Before and after the briefing, I am meeting with staffers from offices across both parties and both chambers. I also have meetings scheduled with [Pew](https://www.pewtrusts.org/en/topics/broadband-access), [Benton](https://www.benton.org/), and [NSF](https://www.nsf.gov/) to discuss next steps on broadband quality measurement research.

## Why I am writing this

I had no formal training in policy engagement before this program. Identifying the right legislative vehicle, framing findings for a non-technical audience, building relationships with staffers: these are not skills that graduate school teaches. UCDC and SSN do, and the difference is that research reaches people who can act on it.

The policy brief is available [here](/pdfs/policy-brief-v18.pdf). If you work on broadband policy, I welcome the conversation: [arpitgupta@ucsb.edu](mailto:arpitgupta@ucsb.edu).
